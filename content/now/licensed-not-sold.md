On August 21 Sony responded to a class action in a California federal court. Its argument, in substance: when you buy a digital game, you're buying a licence to play it, not the game. The licence can end.

This isn't new as a legal position. Every digital storefront's terms have said something like it for years. What's new is a major platform saying it plainly, under oath, while a jailbreak that routes around the licence is spreading.

It sits alongside a bigger movement. The Stop Killing Games petition gathered 1.29 million signatures asking the EU to stop publishers from switching off games people paid for. In June the European Commission declined to pass a law and asked the industry for a voluntary code of conduct by the end of the year.

The plain reading: the question of whether you own your software is being decided right now, mostly by companies, and mostly in their favour. The alternative is not piracy. It's choosing things you can actually own: DRM-free stores, offline-capable software, open source, and increasingly, things you build.
